Acadiana

Cajun Jewish History

Jewish families have been part of Cajun country’s towns since the mid-nineteenth century — merchants and civic builders who found a French-speaking culture that felt familiar, and who left cemeteries, synagogues, and storefronts still visible across Lafayette Parish and the rest of Acadiana.

Vermilionville and a French-speaking affinity

Lafayette began as Vermilionville, founded in 1821 by the French-speaking Acadian Jean Mouton. Jews arrived in the early-to-mid 1800s. The Goldring/Woldenberg Institute of Southern Jewish Life (ISJL) notes that many came from Alsace-Lorraine and were at ease with the language and quasi-French Cajun culture of southwest Louisiana. They were never a large share of the population, but they were not on the margins either: dry-goods stores, opera houses, oil-field real estate, and civic boards all carried Jewish names.

Temple Shalom’s own history page describes the same welcome: newcomers found “an atmosphere… that spoke of opportunity for their skills, products, and services” in a growing French-speaking town. The railroad later thickened the community. By the time Vermilionville was renamed Lafayette in 1884, Jewish merchants were established downtown.

Lafayette: cemetery, congregation, synagogue

Southern Jewish towns often built a cemetery first and a synagogue second. Lafayette followed that pattern.

In 1869, former Louisiana governor Alexandre Mouton donated land at Lee and College Avenues for a Jewish burial ground. Neighbors called it “Hebrew Rest.” In April 1890 the cemetery association was formally chartered as Menachim Aveilim (“Comforting the Mourning”) after a meeting at Falk’s Opera House. That Lafayette cemetery is not the same place as Hebrew Rest Cemetery in Washington, St. Landry Parish.

When was Rodeph Sholom organized? ISJL emphasizes 4 June 1881, when Hyman Lieberman, Joseph Plonsky, Lazarus Levy, Benjamin Falk, Eugene Levy, and others organized the Hebrew Association Rodeph Sholom (“Pursuit of Peace”) to form a congregation. Temple Shalom and some local accounts date the congregation to 1869 — the same year as the cemetery gift — and describe early worship as Orthodox, held in members’ homes. Both dates matter: 1869 marks communal burial and, in local memory, the start of organized Jewish life; 1881 is the documented founding of the Hebrew Association that built the synagogue.

Governor Mouton donated two lots on Lee Avenue for a synagogue (ISJL and Temple Shalom agree this gift came in 1881). After years of bazaars and building appeals, the synagogue rose in 1889. The local paper called it a “tremendous building” for Lafayette’s Israelites. The congregation, founded Orthodox, soon affiliated with Reform Judaism — Temple Shalom says at the time the building opened; ISJL says shortly after the founding. Between 1902 and 1910 the building also hosted Sunday services for Lafayette Baptists.

Charter members of the new temple included Leon Plonsky, Lazarus Levy, Benjamin Falk, Solomon Wise, Joseph Wise, Abraham Haas, Hyman Lieberman, and Mervine (M.) Kahn. For much of its life the congregation relied on Hebrew Union College student rabbis rather than a full-time pulpit. The Sisterhood (organized in 1913 as the Jewish Ladies Aid Society) ran fundraising, wartime relief, and, from 1933, the Sunday school.

The Lee Avenue building was remodeled in 1932, 1953, and 1974. The Heymann Oil Center, developed in the 1950s, drew Jewish families into Lafayette; ISJL estimates about 105 Jews in 1960 and as many as 600 in the area by 1980. Growth also brought people from more traditional backgrounds who were uneasy with classical Reform worship. In December 1973 a group left to form Yeshurun Synagogue. They met first in homes, then built on Kaliste Saloom Road (1975) and, though more conservative in style, also affiliated with Reform and hired HUC student rabbis.

In 1992 the two congregations made peace and merged as Temple Shalom, keeping the historic Lee Avenue sanctuary and adopting looser kosher practice in the kitchen as a compromise. Temple Shalom remains a Reform congregation and one of the oldest active synagogues in Louisiana. It still has no full-time rabbi in the ISJL account, continuing the student-rabbi pattern. The congregation is also custodian of a mid-nineteenth-century Torah scroll saved from the Holocaust.

Parallel communities

Opelousas

Jews were in Opelousas by the 1850s as merchants and farmers, not itinerant peddlers. A cemetery was purchased in 1865. In 1877 the community organized Gemiluth Chassodim (“Acts of Loving Kindness”), meeting in the Humble Cottage Masonic Lodge. Membership stayed small; the earlier congregation faded, and Temple Emanuel was founded in 1929 with about 65 members, absorbing Gemiluth Chassodim. A brick synagogue on land given by the Haas family at South Main Street was dedicated that year (the cornerstone ceremony was 9 June 1929).

Services lapsed by 1942, then revived in 1955. Over later decades many families gravitated to Lafayette. The Temple Emanuel building was sold in 2021 after nearly a century of use. The congregation and the Temple Emanuel / Gemiluth Chassodim Cemetery on Bellevue Street continue. City of Opelousas historical notes and local reporting (St. Landry Now) document the sale and the cemetery’s 2021 formal renaming as Temple Emanuel Jewish Cemetery.

New Iberia

Jewish families arrived in the 1870s. High Holiday worship was held in the Masonic Hall before Congregation Gates of Prayer organized in 1897 and built a brick synagogue at Charles and Weeks streets, dedicated in 1904. Catholics and Jews publicly supported one another’s building campaigns. The congregation bought cemetery land on West Main Street in 1913; until then New Iberia Jews were buried in New Orleans or Lafayette. Gates of Prayer never had a lasting full-time rabbi. It shared clergy at times with Lafayette and Morgan City and still gathers at 109 Weeks Street.

Crowley and Rayne

Jewish merchants helped open the rice prairie. Jacob Frankel was Crowley’s first merchant and an early civic officer; in nearby Rayne, Mervine Kahn’s 1884 store and bank were engines of town growth. Crowley’s Jews worshiped in Lake Charles until Congregation Emmanuel formed in 1904, meeting in the Masonic Temple. The congregation numbered over 100 by 1907, then shrank after World War I. Remaining members from Crowley and Rayne later joined Lafayette.

Kaplan

The town of Kaplan was founded in 1903 by Abrom Kaplan, a Jewish rice miller who bought former plantation land, gave parcels to attract workers, and lobbied for a railroad extension. Kaplan never had its own synagogue or cemetery. Abrom Kaplan served as an officer of Lafayette’s Jewish cemetery association and was buried there. A few Jewish families in town affiliated with Gates of Prayer in New Iberia.

Notable figures

  • Maurice Heymann (b. New Orleans, 1885) built a Lafayette retail empire, opened the city’s first supermarket-style “food center” in 1937, and developed the Heymann Oil Center in 1952 — a move ISJL credits with turning a sleepy agrarian town into a Gulf oil hub. He was Lafayette’s first Civic Cup honoree.
  • Benjamin Falk, a Russian-born dry-goods merchant, was among Lafayette’s leading late-nineteenth-century citizens: alderman, volunteer fireman, oil-mill director, and owner of Falk’s Opera House. He helped start the Lafayette Improvement Association (forerunner of the Chamber of Commerce) and a city high school (1894). His 1901 funeral was described as the largest the town had seen.
  • Lazarus Levy came to Vermilionville in 1878 and built a dry-goods business across from the Bendel stores. His sons Victor, William, and Mose opened Levy Bros. in 1907.
  • Henri Willis Bendel (b. Vermilionville, 1868) became an internationally known milliner and couturier, with stores on Fifth Avenue and in Paris. He was the son of Mary Plonsky Bendel; after William Bendel’s death, Mary married Benjamin Falk.
