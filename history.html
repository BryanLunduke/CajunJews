Acadiana

Timeline of Jewish History in Acadiana

Dated events below follow published sources. Where those sources disagree on a year, both are noted in parentheses.

  1. Early–mid 1800s

    Jewish families settle in Vermilionville (later Lafayette). ISJL notes that many came from Alsace-Lorraine and were at ease with the French-speaking Cajun culture; Temple Shalom describes a town that “spoke of opportunity for their skills, products, and services.”

  2. 1850s

    Jewish merchants and farmers establish themselves in Opelousas, advertising dry goods and other trades in the local press.

  3. 1865

    Jews in Opelousas purchase a cemetery (ISJL). The burial ground on Bellevue Street later continues as the Temple Emanuel / Gemiluth Chassodim Cemetery.

  4. 1866–1867

    Carel Wolff donates land in Washington, St. Landry Parish, for Hebrew Rest Cemetery after his children Samuel and Fannie die of yellow fever in October 1867. This cemetery is distinct from Lafayette’s informal “Hebrew Rest” name.

  5. 1868 / 1877

    Opelousas Jews organize Gemiluth Chassodim (“Acts of Loving Kindness”), meeting in the Humble Cottage Masonic Lodge. ISJL dates the congregation to 1877; the City of Opelousas dates a Hebrew charter of that name to 1868.

  6. 1869

    Former Louisiana governor Alexandre Mouton donated land at Lee and College Avenues for a Jewish burial ground. Neighbors call it “Hebrew Rest.”

  7. 1870s

    Jewish families settle in New Iberia and nearby towns along Bayou Teche, opening dry-goods and merchandise stores.

  8. 1881

    On 4 June, Hyman Lieberman, Joseph Plonsky, Lazarus Levy, Benjamin Falk, Eugene Levy, and others organize the Hebrew Association Rodeph Sholom (“Pursuit of Peace”). Governor Mouton donates two lots on Lee Avenue for a synagogue. (ISJL dates this organization to 1881; Temple Shalom and some local accounts remember a congregation from 1869, the year of the cemetery gift.)

  9. 1884

    Vermilionville is renamed Lafayette. Jewish merchants are already established downtown.

  10. 1889

    The Rodeph Sholom synagogue is built on Lee Avenue. The local paper calls it a “tremendous building” for Lafayette’s Israelites. The congregation, begun as Orthodox, soon affiliates with Reform Judaism (Temple Shalom: at the time the building opened; ISJL: shortly after the association’s founding).

  11. 1890

    The Lafayette cemetery association is chartered as Menachim Aveilim (“Comforting the Mourning”) after a meeting at Falk’s Opera House.

  12. 1897

    Jews who had been worshiping in New Iberia’s Masonic Hall organize Congregation Gates of Prayer and plan a brick synagogue at Charles and Weeks streets.

  13. 1903

    Abrom Kaplan, a Jewish rice miller, founds the town of Kaplan. He gives land to attract workers and lobbies for a railroad extension; the town never has its own synagogue or cemetery.

  14. 1904

    Congregation Emmanuel is formed in Crowley, meeting in the Masonic Temple after years of traveling to Lake Charles for services. Membership later declines, and remaining Crowley and Rayne families join Lafayette.

  15. 1904

    Gates of Prayer’s New Iberia synagogue is dedicated under student rabbi Julian Miller of Hebrew Union College.

  16. 1913

    Gates of Prayer purchases cemetery land on West Main Street in New Iberia. Until then, local Jews were buried in New Orleans or Lafayette.

  17. 1916

    Maurice Heymann, born in New Orleans in 1885 to Polish Jewish father Henry Heymann (born Leon Kopcwiski) and French Jewish mother Caroline Rosenthal, opens his first store in Lafayette.

  18. 1929–1930

    Temple Emanuel is founded in Opelousas with about 65 members, absorbing Gemiluth Chassodim. A brick synagogue on Haas family land at South Main Street is dedicated (cornerstone 9 June 1929; ISJL dates the building to 1930).

  19. 1937

    Heymann opens a “food center” in his Lafayette department store, the city’s first supermarket-style grocery.

  20. 1952

    Maurice Heymann develops the Heymann Oil Center. ISJL credits the project with drawing oil companies to Lafayette and with a sharp rise in the local Jewish population in the decades that follow.

  21. 1960

    Lafayette’s municipal auditorium is completed, envisioned by Mayor Jerome Domengeaux and Herbert Heymann and designed by A. Hays Town (plans 1957). The building is later rededicated as the Heymann Performing Arts Center. ISJL estimates about 105 Jews in Lafayette this year.

  22. 1973

    In December, a group leaves Rodeph Sholom and forms Yeshurun Synagogue, first meeting in members’ homes.

  23. 1975

    Yeshurun builds on Kaliste Saloom Road. The new congregation affiliates with Reform Judaism and hires Hebrew Union College student rabbis.

  24. 1976

    Siblings Herbert and Jacqueline Heymann found the Heymann Foundation in Lafayette to continue the family’s philanthropy in honor of Maurice Heymann and his brother Henry.

  25. 1992

    Rodeph Sholom and Yeshurun merge as Temple Shalom, keeping the historic Lee Avenue sanctuary.

  26. 2021

    The Temple Emanuel synagogue building in Opelousas is sold after nearly a century of use. The congregation continues, and the Bellevue Street cemetery is formally named Temple Emanuel Jewish Cemetery.
